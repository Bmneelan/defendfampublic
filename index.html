For kids & teens

A clean credit file is the most valuable thing a kid owns. It isn't the only thing being targeted.

Quick answer

What are kids and teens actually targeted for? Four distinct things: synthetic identity fraud on an unmonitored credit file, everyday scams inside the apps and games they already use, financial sextortion, and AI-generated fake explicit images. Each needs a different response, covered below.

A lot of the reporting behind these numbers is written about teenagers specifically, but the platforms involved, Roblox, Discord, Instagram, are used by much younger kids too. The same freeze-the-file, use-a-password-manager, talk-before-it-happens guidance applies earlier than most parents assume.

Four threat vectors
VECTOR 01

Social-media impersonation

Cloned profiles harvest trust from friends, then run scams in the victim's name.

VECTOR 02

Credential reuse

One leaked game-forum password unlocks email, banking and everything chained to it.

VECTOR 03

Data-broker sales

Names, ages and addresses are packaged and sold legally, assembling a profile to exploit.

VECTOR 04

Synthetic identity

A real SSN fused with a fake name builds credit for years before anyone notices.

The everyday scams kids and teens actually run into

This is where parent credibility gets built or lost. A warning about identity theft can feel abstract to a kid who's never seen it happen. A warning about the exact Robux scam a friend fell for last week lands immediately, at almost any age.

"Free Robux" apps

Suspected gaming fraud rose 53% year-over-year in 2026; Roblox alone saw ~1.65M attempted attacks. Spin-wheel apps keep players one step from a payout that never comes.

Discord escalation

Roblox chat is filtered; Discord isn't. Predatory adults move conversations there, building rapport over days before asking for credentials or money.

Fake Instagram brand deals

A DM collaboration offer that leads to a phishing link or a "contract" asking for payment or login credentials before anything official happens.

Financial sextortion: one of the fastest-growing threats to teens

NCMEC received more than 50,000 reports of financially motivated sextortion in 2025, an average of 137 a day, up from 36,000 the year before. The pattern: a stranger poses as a peer, moves the conversation to a private app, then extorts payment after getting an image. Reports skew heavily toward boys 14–17. The most important thing to say in advance: paying does not make it stop, and if it's already happened, the teen is not in trouble and not to blame.

AI-generated images: the "nudify" problem

No real image is required: "nudify" apps generate a fake explicit image from an ordinary, already-public photo. The federal TAKE IT DOWN Act (May 2025) requires covered platforms to remove a valid report within 48 hours. NCMEC's free Take It Down service (takeitdown.ncmec.org) fingerprints the image so platforms can detect and remove it without it ever being uploaded anywhere. The same response whether the image is real or AI-generated.

For adults

One reused password is the door to every account you own.

Quick answer

Is a password manager still enough in 2026? Mostly, but it's no longer the strongest option. A unique password per site stops the most common attack outright. The stronger fix, where offered, is a passkey, which removes the reusable password entirely rather than just managing it better.

22%
Of all confirmed 2025 breaches started with stolen or reused credentials (88% of attacks on basic web applications specifically, per Verizon's 2025 DBIR). Separately, 36% of people say one of their own accounts has already been compromised this way (FIDO Alliance, 2025).
◆ Source verified · Verizon 2025 DBIR [4]
88%
of attacks on basic web applications involve stolen or reused credentials. · Verizon 2025 DBIR [4]
The core mechanic

Credential stuffing

A breach leaks your email + password from one site. Bots replay that pair against thousands of banks and retailers automatically. Reuse the password anywhere and the attack succeeds. No "hacking" required.

Emerging · Deepfake video

Liveness checks under attack

Banks increasingly verify big transfers with video selfies. Synthetic-video tooling now defeats some liveness checks, turning "see my face" into a forgeable credential. MFA that isn't tied to a device is the weak point.

The stronger option: passkeys

A passkey does away with the shared secret entirely rather than protecting it better. Nothing stored that a breach can leak, nothing to type that a fake login page can capture. It resists both credential stuffing and phishing in a way password-plus-MFA setups aren't quite. Support is still uneven across sites, so a password manager remains the right fallback everywhere passkeys aren't offered yet.

The guidance

Three priorities, do them in this order

P1

Password manager or passkey

Closes the door credential stuffing walks through. Everything else is secondary to this.

P2

Device-bound MFA

For accounts that don't yet support passkeys. An authenticator app or hardware key resists deepfake and SIM-swap attacks.

P3

Breach monitoring

The safety net that catches a leaked credential before bots get there, not the wall itself.

For elders

The voice asking for money sounds exactly like family.

Quick answer

Is a family safe word enough to stop an AI voice-cloning scam? Not by itself. It stops a scammer who doesn't know the word, but a determined one can talk around it or fake enough distress to avoid answering, which is why calling back on a number you already had saved matters just as much. Why elders are targeted

Trust in the voice

A familiar voice short-circuits skepticism. Cloning needs only seconds of audio from social media.

Manufactured urgency

"I'm in jail / in an accident, don't tell anyone." Panic is the exploit, not technology.

Untraceable payment

Gift cards, wire transfers and crypto are requested precisely because they can't be reversed.

Isolation by design

"Keep this between us" prevents the one thing that stops the scam: a second opinion.

Three moves, in order of impact

01

Set a family safe-word

Not absolute on its own: a determined scammer can talk around it. Pair it with rule two below.

COST: $0

Call back on a saved number

Never the number that called you, that can be spoofed too. Use one you already had.

COST: $0
03

Identity monitoring

For the sandwich generation

If you're watching out for more than one person, start here.

Quick answer

Where do I start if I'm managing this for my kids and my parents at the same time? The core moves are the same at every age, freeze the credit file, use a password manager, agree on a callback rule before there's an emergency, but who's most exposed right now usually decides the order. An unmonitored teen credit file and an aging parent's phone are both live risks; neither should wait for the other.

Pick a starting point
What actually carries across generations

One password manager, several vaults

A shared family plan (1Password, Bitwarden) lets everyone get unique logins without you managing each person's separately.

The same callback rule

"Hang up and call back on a saved number" defeats spoofed calls whether the target is a teenager or a grandparent.

Caregiver-visible banking

True Link and bank-offered caregiver access give a family member view-only or spending-limited visibility without full account control, useful for a parent or an older teen alike.

There's no single "family" checklist on this site by design. The mechanics of a Roblox scam and a cloned-voice scam are different enough that combining them into one page would blur both. Use the three cards above to route to the guidance that actually matches who you're worried about right now.
For small business

The same threats, aimed at your business instead of your household.

Credential stuffing, social engineering, and account takeover don't stop at the front door. A small business runs the identical mechanics as a household, one reused password, one convincing phone call, with higher stakes per incident and fewer people watching for it.

What carries over directly

Credential stuffing

The exact mechanic covered on the Adults page, applied to a business email or admin login instead of a personal account.

Social engineering

The manufactured-urgency playbook that targets elders shows up as vendor-impersonation and invoice fraud in a business inbox.

Deepfake voice & video

The same cloning technology behind family-emergency scams is already being used to impersonate executives authorizing wire transfers.

A dedicated business track, covering the parts that don't carry over (endpoint security, vendor risk, employee training) is in development. If that's what you're here for, the household guidance above is a reasonable starting point today, and a fuller track is coming.
