# DefendFam Content Voice Guide

Read this automatically before touching anything a person will read: homepage copy, threat guides, product cards, comparisons, disclosures, CTAs.

Brad is the author. This is his voice, sourced and preserved, not a style to optimize toward. When intent is unclear, flag it and leave the existing wording rather than guessing.

---

## The checklist (check every edit against this)

1. **No em dashes.** Period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead.
2. **No arrow bullets in body copy.**
3. **Free fix comes before the paid one**, every time, in every section.
4. **Every statistic has a real source** (FTC, FBI/IC3, NCMEC, Verizon DBIR, FIDO Alliance, original research), never a blog citing a blog. If you can't verify it, flag it or placeholder it. Never invent a number.
5. **Explain the mechanic before the recommendation.** How the scam/attack/exposure actually works comes before what to do about it.
6. **Affiliate relationships stay plain**, never softened, never buried at the bottom.
7. **Every recommendation passes:** would we still say this if the company paid us nothing?
8. **Read the whole page after editing, not just your section.** A fix in paragraph two can leave three em dashes sitting in paragraph four.
9. **Final test before publishing:**
   - Did we explain what's actually happening?
   - Did we give the reader the cheapest reasonable solution first?
   - Can we support every factual claim?
   - Does this sound like someone who knows the industry, or something that's read a lot about it?

If the last answer is wrong, rewrite it.

## Reference

### Who's writing

Brad: 25 years in digital marketing, search, and media, including leading search and AI-powered media at McAfee and building the search function at Intuit QuickBooks. That's context, not a résumé to write in. The voice comes through as someone who's worked inside the machinery, knows how these products get marketed, and is willing to separate a useful product from a good sales pitch. Not a cybersecurity company. Not an affiliate site optimizing for a keyword.

### The point of view

Diagnose the threat first. Recommend tools second. Start with what can happen, explain how, gauge how likely or serious it actually is, show what someone can do about it, then, if a product genuinely helps, name one. Sometimes the right answer costs nothing: a setting, a credit freeze, a conversation with a kid. The recommendation follows the evidence, not the other way around.

### What Brad's voice actually sounds like

He works through a problem instead of announcing a conclusion up front:

"There are really two risks here."
"This sounds worse than it usually is."
"This is one of the cases where I'd pay for the added protection."
"You can solve most of this without buying anything."
"The company's technically correct, but that leaves out an important part of the story."

Opinions are fine. So is uncertainty, stated plainly, when the evidence doesn't fully support a clean answer.

The transferable pattern isn't the specific "X or Y" interview format this came from, it's the sentence-level habits underneath: a decisive stance stated plainly before the justification arrives, comfort landing on "both" or "interdependent" when that's the honest answer rather than treating it as a dodge, analytical metaphors that do real explanatory work rather than decoration, and short blunt sentences sitting next to longer explanatory ones on purpose.

### Free first, always

1. What can someone do themselves, free?
2. What do they already have through a bank, employer, carrier, OS, or browser?
3. What does a paid product add on top of that?
4. Is the addition actually worth paying for?

If a free answer solves it, say that plainly, even if DefendFam makes money on the alternative.

### No manufactured fear

Don't make an unusual threat sound universal. Don't take the worst-case number in a report and imply it's everyone's risk. Words like "terrifying," "alarming," "massive," "exploding" need to be earned by the evidence, not reached for. Separate possibility from probability, inconvenience from real harm. The goal is informed action, not anxiety.

### Product recommendations

Evaluate, don't advertise: what it does, what it doesn't solve, who needs it, who doesn't, what a comparable free protection might already cover, what it costs. A feature only matters if it changes the outcome for the person using it, don't repeat vendor language as if a dashboard or an alert is automatically a benefit.

### Sentence-level patterns to avoid

- "It's not just X, it's Y." "This isn't about X, it's about Y." "Whether you're X, Y, or Z..."
- Rule-of-three padding where two things, or four things, would be honest
- Stock transitions: "That said," "Here's the thing," "At the end of the day," "The reality is"
- Empty intensifiers stacked up: "genuinely," "truly," "actually," "incredibly", usually a sign the sentence needs a better verb, not a stronger adverb
- Generic headers ("Understanding the Problem," "Key Considerations") instead of headers that state an actual claim ("A credit freeze stops a different part of the problem")

### Page structure

The site's component system (Quick Answer box, stat callout, numbered guidance, software cards) is intentional and shouldn't be dissolved in the name of "variety," that's real information architecture doing work for scanning readers. What should vary is the prose within those components: don't let every paragraph inside them fall into the identical rhythm. A technical topic might want numbered steps; a narrative threat explainer might not. Let the material decide the shape, without abandoning the shell that makes the site scannable.

### Editing existing copy

Read the surrounding page before changing anything, match the rhythm that's already there instead of overwriting it with a generic editorial voice. Preserve wording that sounds intentionally Brad even if a smoother version is available. Don't convert prose to bullets unless bullets genuinely help. Don't add intros, summaries, or transitions that don't carry information.

### The litmus test

If a sentence could sit unchanged on Norton, McAfee, Aura, LifeLock, or Experian's site, it's not sounding like DefendFam yet, rewrite it.
