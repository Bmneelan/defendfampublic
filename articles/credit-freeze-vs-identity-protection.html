Credit freeze or paid identity protection: which do you need?

A freeze is free and blocks the most damaging kind of identity theft. Here is what paid plans add, and who they actually fit.

By Brad NeelanUpdated October 1, 20267 min read
The short version

Freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. It is free, takes about fifteen minutes, and blocks new accounts in your name. A paid plan is worth it mainly if you manage protection for several family members or want someone else to handle cleanup if theft happens.

Most people who ask this question do not need to pay anything. So here is the answer first, plainly.

A credit freeze is free by federal law, takes about fifteen minutes across all three bureaus, and blocks the most damaging thing an identity thief does, which is open new accounts in your name. Nothing you can buy does that particular job better.

What paid services add is not stronger protection against new accounts. It is monitoring, alerts, and someone to call when something goes wrong. That is worth money to some households and not to others. The rest of this page explains how to tell which one you are.

What a credit freeze actually does

A freeze restricts access to your credit file. When a lender cannot pull your report, it will not approve a new account. That is why a freeze stops new-account fraud at the moment it would happen, instead of telling you about it afterward.

Since September 21, 2018, federal law has made freezes free at all three bureaus, including for children under 16.

Four things people get wrong about freezes

It does not affect your credit score. A freeze changes who can look at your file, not what it says.

It does not stop you using your existing cards. Your accounts work normally. Only new credit applications are affected.

You can lift it when you need to. Applying for a mortgage, a car loan, or a new card? Lift the freeze, then put it back. When you ask online or by phone, the bureau has to lift it within one hour.

A freeze is not the same as a lock. Bureaus also sell "credit lock" products that behave similarly, but a lock can carry a monthly fee and is governed by a company contract rather than federal law. If you want the free version guaranteed by law, ask for a freeze.

What a fraud alert does, and when to use one instead

A fraud alert does not restrict access. It tells businesses checking your credit to verify your identity first, which usually means calling you. You place it at one bureau and that bureau notifies the other two.

An initial alert lasts one year and can be renewed for free. If you have experienced identity theft and filed a report at IdentityTheft.gov, you can get an extended alert that lasts seven years.

Alerts are lighter than freezes. An alert suits you if you apply for credit often enough that lifting a freeze would be a nuisance. A freeze suits you if you rarely open new accounts, which describes most households.

So what are you paying for?

Paid identity protection usually bundles four things. Judged one at a time:

Credit and dark web monitoring. Tells you after your information shows up somewhere it should not. Useful, but it detects rather than prevents, and a freeze already blocks the outcome most alerts warn about. Free breach-notification tools cover part of this at no cost.

Restoration support. A caseworker who helps you unwind identity theft: the calls, the affidavits, the disputes. This is the part with real value, and the part nobody thinks about until they need it. Untangling a stolen identity across several institutions can take weeks.

Insurance, usually up to a headline amount. Read what it actually covers. Typically that is documented out-of-pocket costs and sometimes lost wages, not the stolen money itself. The headline number is a policy limit, not a payout.

Coverage for family members. Children and older parents under one plan. This is the strongest argument for paying if you are the person in the household who would be handling everyone else's problem anyway.

The honest decision rule

Freeze only, no subscription. You rarely open new credit, you can handle a few hours of phone calls if something goes wrong, and nobody else depends on you to manage this. This is most people.

Freeze plus a paid plan. You manage this for several people, including a parent or a child. Or you have been through identity theft before and know what the cleanup costs in time. Or your Social Security number was in a breach and you want monitoring while you watch for fallout. In those cases you are buying the restoration desk, so compare plans on restoration and family coverage, not on the size of the insurance number.

Neither. There is no version of this where doing nothing is the right answer. The freeze is free.

If you decide to pay, compare on these

Ask every plan the same four questions: who handles restoration and whether it is a named caseworker, which family members are covered and at what ages, what the insurance actually reimburses, and whether you can cancel without a phone call. A plan that answers those clearly is worth considering. A plan that leads with the insurance headline usually is not.
