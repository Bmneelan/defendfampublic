The delivery text that isn't from the post office

Fake package texts were the most reported text scam in the US in the FTC's 2024 analysis. Here's how to spot one, and what to do if you already tapped.

By Brad NeelanUpdated October 7, 20266 min read
The short version

Don't use the link in a delivery text you weren't expecting.

If you're actually waiting for a package, check the order or tracking in the store or carrier's own app. Report the text as junk or forward it to 7726, then delete it.

If you already gave the site a card number or other personal information, that's different. Deal with that now.

"Your package could not be delivered. Update your address here."

The annoying part is that you probably are waiting for a package.

That's why this scam works.

Fake package delivery messages were the most reported text scam in the FTC's 2024 analysis, and most pretended to be the US Postal Service. The text sends you to a site that looks close enough to USPS, UPS, FedEx or another carrier and asks you to fix some small problem.

Often there's a "redelivery fee" of a dollar or two. And that isn't the point, it's really more about your credit card.

In some reported cases, the fake site also asks for information like your Social Security number.

How to spot one

You didn't ask for the text

This is especially useful with USPS.

USPS says its tracking texts are sent after you request tracking for a specific package. Think about how the USPS doesn't randomly text you about a package and ask you to fix something through a link.

So if you never asked USPS for tracking updates and suddenly USPS is texting you about an address problem, that's a pretty good answer.

The text wants you to solve the problem from the text

Don't investigate a delivery problem using the contact information the message gave you.

Open Amazon, Target, Walmart, USPS, UPS, FedEx or wherever you actually ordered from and look there.

If there's really a problem with the package, it will still be a problem when you arrive through the front door instead of the link someone texted you.

There's a tiny fee and a reason to hurry

$1.37 for redelivery.

$2.04 in unpaid postage.

Pay today or the package goes back.

It's small enough that paying it can feel easier than thinking about it. That's the idea to the scammer. Get the transaction.

Nothing quite connects to what you can spot

No retailer is seen and there is no order number you recognize. No tracking number that means anything to you.

Just a vague package that somehow desperately needs your credit card.

Seems obvious but we all get busy and become reactive.

What to do with the text

  1. Don't tap the link or reply.

    Just don't use it, tap it, click it or give the signal to the scammer.

  2. Check the package somewhere else.

    Open the store or carrier app yourself, or type the site into your browser. Use the tracking information from the order you actually placed.

  3. Report it.

    On iPhone or Android, use Report Junk or Report Spam if it's available. You can also forward the text to 7726.

  4. Delete it.

    That's it. It doesn't deserve more of your afternoon.

You can also report scams to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. USPS impersonation scams can be reported to the US Postal Inspection Service.

If you already tapped

First, don't turn one tap into a stressor.

What matters much more is what happened after the tap.

You entered a card number

Call the number on the back of the card and tell the issuer what happened.

Don't wait to see whether a fraudulent charge appears. Ask whether they want to replace the card.

You entered your Social Security number or other identity information

This moves from "bad text" to identity-theft territory.

Freeze your credit with all three major credit bureaus. It's free and makes it much harder for someone to open a new account in your name. This is one of the core ideas we suggest to everyone since it will prevent a lot of struggle.

Our guide to credit freezes walks through it.

You entered a password

Change it on that account.

If you used the same password anywhere else, change it there too. Then turn on two-step verification if the account offers it.

You didn't enter anything

Close the page.

Delete the text.

For most people, that's the end of it and no more time is wasted.

The other text you'll see: "wrong number"

Not every scam bothers with a fake package.

I got this one on September 24, 2026:

There's no link. No fake USPS logo. No urgent payment.

That's what makes this version interesting.

The FTC also lists "wrong number" messages among its most reported text scams. The opening is deliberately harmless: hello, is this Susan, are we still meeting for dinner, who is this?

You answer because correcting a wrong number feels like normal human behavior.

Then the conversation keeps going.

Eventually the stranger becomes unusually friendly. In some cases it develops into a romantic relationship. In others, an investment opportunity somehow appears.

The scam isn't in the first text. The first text just gets you into the conversation.

Same rule: don't reply. Report it as spam and delete it.

The rule I'd give the family

You don't need everyone in the house to become an expert in phishing domains.

Give them one rule:

We don't fix delivery problems from a text. We go to the place we ordered it and check there.

That works for your kids.

It works for your parents.

And frankly, it works for you when you're distracted and Amazon has three things arriving on the same day.
