The delivery text that isn't from the post office

Fake package texts were the most reported text scam in the US last year. How to spot one, and what to do if you already tapped.

By the DefendFam teamUpdated October 1, 20265 min read
The short version

Fake package delivery texts were the most reported text scam in the US in 2024. Do not tap the link. Check tracking in the carrier's own app, forward the text to 7726, and delete it. If you entered card details, call your card issuer today.

"Your package could not be delivered. Update your address here." It arrives when you are probably expecting something, which is exactly the point.

Fake delivery texts were the most reported text scam in the US in 2024, according to the FTC. Most pretend to be the US Postal Service. The link goes to a site that looks like the real one and asks for a small "redelivery fee." The fee is not the goal. Your card number is, and sometimes your Social Security number too.

How to spot it

The postal service does not text you out of nowhere. USPS says it only sends texts when you have signed up for tracking updates yourself, and those texts do not ask you to pay or enter personal details through a link.

The link is the giveaway. A real carrier link goes to the carrier's own domain. Scam links use look-alike addresses, extra words, or shortened links. If you have to study the address to decide, treat it as fake.

A small fee with a deadline. A dollar or two, due today, or the package goes back. The small amount is designed to feel too minor to question.

It doesn't match anything. No tracking number you recognize, no store name, no order. Real delivery notices tie to a specific order.

What to do with the text

  1. Do not tap the link, and do not reply.

    Not even "STOP." Replying confirms your number is active.

  2. Check the real tracking yourself.

    Open the carrier's app, or type the carrier's address into your browser, and enter the tracking number from your order confirmation email.

  3. Forward the text to 7726.

    That spells SPAM. Major US carriers use these reports to block the sender.

  4. Report it, then delete it.

    At reportfraud.ftc.gov. USPS-themed texts can also go to the Postal Inspection Service at spam@uspis.gov.

If you already tapped the link

Tapping alone is usually not the damage. What you typed in afterward decides what to do next.

You entered a card number. Call the number on the back of that card, report the charge as fraud, and ask for a new card. Do it today, not when a strange charge appears.

You entered your Social Security number or date of birth. Freeze your credit at all three bureaus. It is free and blocks new accounts in your name. Our guide to credit freezes walks through it.

You entered a password. Change it on that account and anywhere else you reused it, and turn on two-step verification.

You entered nothing. Close the page and delete the text. Keep your phone's operating system updated so known problems stay patched.

Share this one

This scam lands on every phone in a household, kids and grandparents included, and it spikes when online orders do. The rule is short enough to text to the whole family: we never pay delivery fees from a text, we check tracking in the app.
