Whole family

What the FBI's 2025 crime report means for families

Americans reported $20.9 billion in internet crime losses last year. The numbers that matter for a household, and the free steps they point to.

By the DefendFam teamUpdated October 1, 20266 min read
The short version

Americans reported $20.9 billion lost to internet crime in 2025, up 26 percent. People 60 and older lost the most, $7.7 billion. The protections that matter most are free: a family rule for money requests, a safe word, and a credit freeze for everyone.

Every spring the FBI publishes what Americans reported losing to internet crime the year before. The 2025 report, released in April 2026, is the largest yet. Most coverage leads with the headline total. This page pulls out what is actually useful for a household.

The FTC's separate count, which covers consumer fraud more broadly, found the same direction: a record $15.9 billion reported lost in 2025, up from $12.5 billion in 2024. The two datasets overlap, so their totals should not be added together.

Older family members carry the biggest losses

People 60 and older reported $7.7 billion in losses, roughly a 60 percent increase over 2024 and more than any other age group. That is not because older adults are careless. It is because they are deliberately targeted: they are more likely to have savings, more likely to answer the phone, and more likely to be alone when the call comes.

The practical takeaway is not to lecture a parent about scams. It is to set up a family rule in advance, so that any request for money, gift cards, crypto, or a wire transfer gets a call to one other relative first. Our guide to gift card scams covers how that conversation goes.

Investment and crypto scams usually start on social media or with a "wrong number" text that turns into a friendship, then a can't-miss opportunity. Any stranger who steers the conversation toward an investment platform or a crypto kiosk is running a scam.

Tech and customer support scams start with a pop-up, a call, or a fake fraud alert claiming your computer or account is compromised. Real support teams do not ask for remote access to your computer, gift cards, or a transfer to a "safe" account.

Romance scams build trust over weeks or months before the first request for money. The first request is the moment to stop.

AI is now in the numbers

For the first time, the report tags losses tied to AI: $893 million, including cloned voices in "family member in trouble" calls and deepfakes used to sell fake investments. People 60 and older accounted for $352 million of that. The real figure is almost certainly higher, since most victims cannot tell AI was involved.

The defense is low-tech. Agree on a family safe word that a caller has to know, and hang up and call back on a number you already have whenever a familiar voice asks for money.

What to do with all this

  1. Make the family rule.

    No money, gift cards, crypto, or transfers because of a call or message without checking with one other family member first.

  2. Pick a safe word.

    Something a stranger could not guess from social media. Use it whenever someone claims to be family and needs money fast.

  3. Freeze everyone's credit.

    Free at all three bureaus, including for kids. See our credit freeze guide.

  4. Report anything that happens.

    At IC3.gov for internet crime and ReportFraud.ftc.gov for scams. Reports are how these numbers exist, and how investigators connect cases.
