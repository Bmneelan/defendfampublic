Gift card scams: what to do if you already paid

Why scammers demand gift cards, how the call usually goes, and the steps that sometimes get the money back.

By the DefendFam teamUpdated October 1, 20265 min read
The short version

No legitimate business or government agency accepts gift cards as payment. If you already paid, call the company on the card immediately and keep the card and receipt. Some money can be frozen if you move fast.

If someone calls, texts, or emails and tells you to pay with a gift card, it is a scam. Not usually. Always.

That single rule settles most of these situations before they start, because no real business, government agency, utility, court, or police department takes gift cards as payment. There is no exception in the fine print. A caller who insists otherwise is telling you what they are.

Scammers like gift cards for a simple reason. Once someone reads the numbers off the back of a card to a stranger, the money can be spent within minutes, and it moves through no system built to claw it back. A wire transfer leaves a trail at a bank. A credit card charge can be disputed. A gift card code redeemed by someone overseas is just spent.

How the call usually goes

Almost every gift card scam follows the same three beats.

First, they create an emergency. Your Social Security number has been suspended. There is a warrant out. Your grandson is in jail. Your computer is infected. Your power will be shut off within the hour. The details change. The purpose does not: to move you from thinking to complying.

Second, they keep you on the phone. This is the tell that matters most. The caller asks you to stay on the line while you drive to the store, and tells you not to discuss it with the cashier, or to say the cards are gifts if anyone asks. No real agency has ever needed you on a call while you drive to buy gift cards.

Third, they ask for the numbers. Not the card itself. The long number and the PIN under the scratch-off strip, read aloud or sent as a photo. At that moment the money leaves.

If you already paid

Gift card money is sometimes recoverable if you move fast. Do these in order.

  1. Call the gift card company right away.

    The brand on the card, not the store where you bought it. Tell them the card was used in a scam and ask them to freeze any remaining balance. The FTC keeps a current list of company contact numbers at ftc.gov/giftcards.

  2. Keep the card and the receipt.

    The card carries the numbers the company needs to trace the transaction, and the receipt proves when and where you bought it. People throw these away out of embarrassment, and it can cost them a refund.

  3. Report it to the FTC.

    At reportfraud.ftc.gov. A report will not return your money by itself, but these reports are how investigators see which scams are running and where.

  4. Tell one person you trust.

    Shame keeps victims quiet, and quiet victims get targeted again, often by someone claiming they can recover the first loss for a fee. That second call is also a scam.

If you did not pay

Hang up. Do not call back on the number that called you. If the caller claimed to be a company or agency you actually use, look up their real number yourself, from a bill, a card, or their official site, and ask whether anything is wrong.

If it arrived as a text, forward it to 7726, which spells SPAM on a keypad. Major US carriers use those reports to block the senders. Then delete it.

The one conversation worth having with your family

Gift card scams work best on people who are alone with the problem. The most effective protection is not a product. It is an agreement made in advance, while nothing is happening: nobody in the family buys gift cards because of a phone call without first calling one other family member.

That rule costs nothing, takes one conversation, and breaks the isolation the scam depends on.
